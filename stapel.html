the classes

The volumes class every trade under six heads, but only ever as a place's total — no single good is tagged with its class anywhere in the source. These are read off the names of the goods, which places about nine in ten of them. The rest fall to nicht zugeordnet, shown by default so nothing quietly vanishes. A class belongs to a good and to none of its lines, so here it takes whole stacks out of the yard rather than thinning the ones standing in it — the figure beside each is how many goods it holds.

the places

—

Every place standing in the yard, biggest Werth first. Striking one out takes it from every stack, not only this one — the long tail is what makes a stack unwieldy, and a floor cuts it without having to choose by hand. A place the page never valued is not small, only unknown, so a dash is never cut by the floor.

the top of the stack

The one face that is not a column of figures, so it carries what belongs to the whole stack and to nothing narrower: which good it is, and off which printing it was read. It is set to carry nothing for now — a stack cut by good has not settled what its top should say, and a blank one says nothing untrue. The sum and the ships were tried on it and taken off long before that: a Werth belongs with the figures on the working faces, not over them. Worked out on der Deckel →, which sets this same face on its own, and still sets it for a place.

the faces as files

—

One png a printed side, at the size the brick actually is: a long face is 196 × 45 mm, a small one 95 × 45, the bed 196 × 95. The top comes off with the course that carries it — one to a stack, since every other bed in a yard is under a brick — and it is set even where the yard is standing with a bare one. The good's name is one drawing hung on every course, so it comes off once and not once a course; a face with nothing on it is not written at all.

They arrive as separate downloads — a browser will ask once whether it may save several files.

what is on which face

A row is one line bent round a corner. It begins on the small face, 95 × 45 — the place, flush left, run out on leader dots to the arris — and finishes on the long face, 196 × 45, where the dots pick up again and carry the eye to the quantity in its own measure and then to the Werth, flush right. Of a brick's four upright faces those are the two that meet at the near edge, the small one on the left and the long one on the right, so the whole reading stands in front of anyone who does not move.

Both figures are on the one face on purpose. A quantity and a value are the two halves of one statement — 800,677 Ld'or means nothing without the pounds it was paid for — and nothing is served by making someone walk round a stack to put them together. It costs the place's name the room the figures take, so a long name is clipped rather than allowed to reach the arris: the dots have to start somewhere or the line does not read as a line.

the bed, 196 × 95 — only the topmost course carries it, and here it carries nothing.
the far end, 95 × 45, and the back, 196 × 45 — the same pair again, round the other corner, bare unless the good's name is put on them; then it repeats down every course of the stack, the way a title repeats down the spines of a run of volumes.

Printed on both corners that far pair carries the next three places instead — the small face their names, the long face their figures, read the same way round the same kind of edge — so a brick holds six and the stack comes out half as high. Nothing is lost but the standing still: no one in front of it sees more than half of who sent the good, and the name has nowhere left to go.
